*Part 3 of **AI, As I See It**, a personal series on what AI agents are really changing about how we build software — and what they aren't.*

> Once code stops being the constraint, the job itself changes shape. If the machine can produce the code, and increasingly check it too, what exactly are you being paid for? This is my answer.

## From Typing to Taste

As models get better at writing and reviewing code, the "typing character by character" part of engineering stops being the job. What is left is taste, judgment, and orchestration.

I find that clarifying, though I never really counted writing code as the engineering part in the first place. As a field we quietly conflated the two, because typing was the visible, effortful thing to point at. But to me the actual work was always the rest — deciding what to build, whether it is right, and how the pieces fit. Take the typing away and you are not left with a smaller job. You are left with the job, finally without the noise.

## The Bottleneck Didn't Disappear, It Moved

I made this same point back in Part 2. Once coding is no longer the bottleneck, the constraint does not vanish — it just moves elsewhere in the software development lifecycle, into planning, validation, architectural design.

The role that replaces "typist" is closer to "steering." You are directing capable systems toward a goal and correcting them, rather than producing every keystroke yourself. The skill is no longer speed of output. It is the quality of direction.

## Make It a Reflex

If I had to name one habit that separates people who get value from these tools from people who do not, it is this: the distance between hitting a problem and reaching for an agent should be as small as possible.

A lot of developers are still holding out, though. They stay wary of AI, convinced that writing the code themselves is the only way to really understand it. I get the instinct, but I think it mistakes the typing for the understanding.

It is the difference between "we have access to these tools" and "reaching for them is the default reflex." The first is availability. The second is fluency. Only the second actually changes how work gets done — and it is a habit you build, not a policy you announce.

## Your Name Is Still on the PR

Here is the part I think matters most, and the part that is easy to lose in the excitement. Capability can be delegated. Responsibility cannot.

Your name still goes on the pull request. You are still accountable for what reaches production. An agent can generate the change, but it cannot own the consequences of shipping it. That ownership is exactly why taste and judgment are not soft skills here — they are the job. If you are accountable for the output, you had better be able to evaluate it.

## Scaling Ambition, Not Shrinking Teams

The point of any of this is not to do the same work with fewer people. That is the most boring possible use of it. The point is to let the people you already have be far more ambitious — a small team attempting things that used to need a large one.

That reframes the whole "10X" conversation from Part 1. The win is not fewer engineers writing the same software. It is the same engineers attempting things that were previously out of reach. Whether that materializes depends entirely on whether the engineering discipline scales with the code volume — which is the open question this series keeps circling.

## A Prototype Is Not Production

And an honest caution to close on: a prototype is not production. AI makes it trivial to reach something that *works* on a demo. It does not, by itself, give you architectural stability, performance under load, or the thousand quiet decisions that separate a demo from a system people depend on.

That gap is still human work. Arguably it is *becoming* the human work.
