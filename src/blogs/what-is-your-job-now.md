*Part 3 of **Fast Code, Slow Engineering**, a short series on what AI agents are actually speeding up — and what they aren't.*

> I recently watched a talk by Farhan Thawar, Shopify's head of engineering, called "What Is Your Job Now?" It lines up almost exactly with where this series has been heading — that once code stops being the constraint, the job itself changes shape. These are my notes, and where I think he is right.

## From Typing to Taste

His core claim is simple: as models get better at writing *and* reviewing code, the "typing character by character" part of engineering stops being the job. What is left is taste, judgment, and orchestration.

I find this framing clarifying. For a long time we quietly conflated "writing code" with "engineering," because writing code was the visible, effortful part. Take the typing away and you are left with the part that was always the actual work — deciding what to build, whether it is right, and how the pieces fit.

## The Bottleneck Didn't Disappear, It Moved

This is the same point I made in Part 2, and it was reassuring to hear it from someone running engineering at that scale. When coding is no longer the bottleneck, the constraint moves elsewhere in the software development lifecycle — planning, validation, architectural design.

The role that replaces "typist" is closer to "AI steering." You are directing capable systems toward a goal and correcting them, rather than producing every keystroke yourself. The skill is no longer speed of output. It is the quality of direction.

## Be "AI Reflexive"

One idea I had not heard framed this way: Shopify apparently asks its people to be *AI reflexive*. The distance between hitting a problem and reaching for an AI tool to help with it should be as small as possible.

I like this because it is a habit, not a policy. It is the difference between "we have access to these tools" and "reaching for them is the default reflex." The first is availability; the second is fluency. Only the second actually changes how work gets done.

## Your Name Is Still on the PR

Here is the part I think matters most, and the part that is easy to lose in the excitement. Capability can be delegated. Responsibility cannot.

The engineer's name still goes on the pull request. The engineer is still accountable for what reaches production. An agent can generate the change, but it cannot own the consequences of shipping it. That ownership is exactly why taste and judgment are not soft skills here — they are the job. If you are accountable for the output, you had better be able to evaluate it.

## Scaling Ambition, Not Shrinking Teams

The framing I appreciated most was his refusal to treat AI as a headcount-reduction tool. The point is not to do the same work with fewer people. It is to let the people you have be far more ambitious — his image was letting 3,000 engineers operate like 100,000.

That reframes the whole "10X" conversation from Part 1. The win is not fewer engineers writing the same software. It is the same engineers attempting things that were previously out of reach. Whether that materializes depends entirely on whether the engineering discipline scales with the code volume — which is the open question this series keeps circling.

## A Prototype Is Not Production

And a necessary caution to close on: a prototype is not production. AI makes it trivial to reach something that *works* on a demo. It does not, by itself, give you architectural stability, performance under load, or the thousand quiet decisions that separate a demo from a system people depend on.

That gap is still human work. Arguably it is *becoming* the human work.
